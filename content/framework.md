# CORE: Catechesis for Open Reflection and Engagement

Detailed planning edition for Mark Baldwin Smith

27 October 2026 · Draft 0.3 · Adult formation · 24 sessions

## G1 Purpose and scope

Build a free, openly editable Catholic catechesis that helps adults encounter Jesus Christ, understand the faith, participate in the Church and practise Christian discipleship. Optional printed books should make that same course convenient to use. All essential teaching, facilitator guidance, answers, accessibility adaptations and printable worksheets remain available without payment.

The proposed audience is adults enquiring about Catholicism, returning Catholics and practising Catholics who want firmer foundations. No prior theological education is assumed. This is a framework for writing a course, not a completed catechism, an approved sacramental preparation programme or a substitute for parish accompaniment. An adult initiation team may adapt it within its own diocesan process; completing 24 meetings does not establish readiness for reception or a sacrament. Baptised candidates and unbaptised catechumens have different pastoral and sacramental needs.

The structure brings together the Catechism's four pillars: profession of faith, sacramental life, moral life and prayer. Prayer begins in the first session and continues throughout; moral conversion and service also begin before their dedicated sessions. Jesus' life, death and resurrection hold the subjects together. John Paul II's Catechesi tradendae 5 and Benedict XVI's Deus caritas est 1 provide the governing catechetical orientation [R1, R2].

Use a recognisably Catholic voice that welcomes honest questions. Explain technical terms immediately; avoid treating confidence, emotional intensity or agreement in a discussion as a measure of faith. Teach the Church's doctrine accurately while respecting participants who are uncertain or disagree. Include Latin and Eastern Catholic perspectives without presenting local Latin practices as universal rules.

**Course outcomes.** Participants should be able to explain the Creed in plain language; describe the seven sacraments and seek appropriate parish guidance; begin a sustainable prayer practice; distinguish worship from veneration; explain ecclesial authority without confusing it with personal perfection; and connect conscience, charity and hope to everyday decisions. Assess explanations and practical navigation, never the sincerity of someone's relationship with God.

**Facilitator boundary.** Group leaders teach and accompany. They do not hear confessions, adjudicate marriages, diagnose spiritual or psychological conditions, promise secrecy about safeguarding disclosures, or determine sacramental eligibility on their own. Identify a parish contact and safeguarding route before starting. Sensitive questions can be submitted anonymously or discussed privately with an appropriate person.

## G2 Course format and session method

Run 24 weekly sessions of 90 minutes, ideally in four blocks of six with breaks between blocks. Allow another 15 minutes for optional conversation afterwards. Groups of roughly 6–12 are a planning assumption, not a requirement. Reading between sessions is optional and normally takes no more than ten minutes. The framework's lists are writing briefs for leaders; learners receive a much shorter handout.

| Element | Minutes | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encounter | 10 | Welcome, recap and an optional brief prayer or observation of an object or image |
| Question | 10 | One recognisable human question and space for participants' initial responses |
| Jesus | 10 | Read the selected biblical passage aloud and explain its context |
| Discovery | 20 | Teach the three essential ideas, with a pause halfway for clarification |
| Depth | 15 | Read one short historical quotation and address the three prepared FAQs |
| Practice | 15 | Complete the specified activity in pairs, alone or through discussion |
| Return and sending | 10 | Check understanding, offer one weekly practice and close with prayer |

Every session below specifies an observable outcome, essential teaching, Scripture and Catechism references, a historical voice, three FAQs with starter answers, a 15-minute activity, an exit check and an optional weekly practice. This makes each a commissionable writing brief. The quotation identifiers Q1–Q15 refer to the actual quotations in G4. CCC means Catechism of the Catholic Church; the numbers identify paragraphs, not pages. The listed ranges are reference material for writers, not compulsory learner reading.

**Essential and deeper learning.** Teach the three numbered ideas in each session first. Define no more than three new specialist words in the main learner handout. Put historical disputes, additional quotations and specialist explanations in a clearly optional further-reading page. A facilitator may say, “I need to check that,” and return with a sourced answer. Use the same approach for questions whose answers depend on a person's circumstances.

**Materials and preparation.** All sessions need the free learner sheet, a Bible or supplied passage references, paper and pens; speaking or listening can replace writing. Leaders read the named CCC paragraphs and quotation context, prepare a plain-language explanation and check the activity in advance. Additional objects are named below and may always be replaced by a printed description. A trained lead and a second helper are useful where pastoral needs or accessibility require them.

**Alternative pacing.** In a 60-minute meeting use 5 minutes welcome, 5 question, 10 Scripture, 15 teaching, 10 quotation and FAQs, 10 practice, 5 sending. Offer further questions afterwards. If a group needs more time, split the session; never omit entire sacraments or the moral life to meet an arbitrary deadline. A six-session introduction may signpost the full course but should not be presented as equivalent formation.

## Session 01 Encountering Jesus and asking questions

**Question and outcome.** What are we looking for? Participants can describe the Christian proclamation in their own words and name one question they are free to explore.

**Essential teaching.** 1. God acts first in love; faith is a freely given response. 2. Jesus' life, death and resurrection open communion with God and a new way of living. 3. Catechesis involves understanding, worship, relationships and action; it does not require pretending to have certainty.

**Texts and voice.** Luke 24:13–35; CCC 1–3, 142–165; Q1 Augustine on restless hearts and Q2 Benedict XVI on encounter. Writer references: R1, R2 and the CCC index R0.

**FAQs.** Do I have to be Catholic? No; enquiring and observing are welcome. Is faith opposed to reason? Catholic teaching sees faith and reason as compatible; some questions remain difficult and deserve investigation. What if the Church has hurt me? You may name that, decline to discuss it or seek support elsewhere; no one must share personal experiences to participate.

**Practice and check.** Spend 3 minutes quietly identifying hopes, 7 minutes collecting anonymous questions, and 5 minutes agreeing group expectations. Expected output: a question list and a shared agreement to listen, allow passing and avoid pressure. Exit check: explain the good news without starting with a list of rules.

**During the week.** Read a short Gospel encounter or sit quietly for two minutes and address God honestly. Facilitator prepares an anonymous question box and the local support contacts.

## Session 02 Scripture Tradition and learning the faith

**Question and outcome.** How do we know what Christians believe? Participants can locate a biblical passage and a CCC paragraph and distinguish apostolic Tradition from a changeable custom.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Revelation is God's self-communication, fulfilled in Christ. 2. Scripture and apostolic Tradition transmit the one deposit of faith; the Church's teaching office serves that Word. 3. Biblical interpretation considers genre, historical setting, the unity of Scripture and the Church's faith. The Old Testament remains Christian Scripture; Israel's story must not be reduced to a discarded preliminary.

**Texts and voice.** 2 Timothy 3:14–17; Luke 24:27; CCC 50–100, 101–141; Dei Verbum 9–12; Q3 John Paul II on the purpose of catechesis. References R0, R1, R3.

**FAQs.** Do Catholics read the Bible? Yes; Scripture is central to worship and should nourish personal life. Is every biblical passage the same kind of history? No; poetry, parable, law, letters and historical narrative require appropriate reading. Can a custom change? Some disciplines and customs can; the apostolic faith is not simply a collection of local preferences.

**Practice and check.** With a Bible contents page and a CCC sample, spend 5 minutes locating Luke 15, 5 locating CCC 80 and 5 comparing a biblical text, a doctrinal summary and a local custom. Expected output: a correctly labelled three-item sheet. Exit check: explain why a quotation needs context.

**During the week.** Read Luke 15 and note one question. Offer the passage read aloud so the task does not depend on print literacy.

## Session 03 The Creed and the Holy Trinity

**Question and outcome.** Who is the God Christians confess? Participants can identify the Creed's Trinitarian structure and explain one God in three distinct divine Persons without describing three gods or three disguises.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The Apostles' and Nicene Constantinopolitan Creeds summarise the Church's faith; the latter developed through the fourth-century councils. 2. Father, Son and Holy Spirit are coequal and coeternal; each is fully the one God, not a third of God. 3. We are invited by grace into communion with the Trinity. “Person” here is not simply the modern idea of three independent individuals. Divine actions towards creation are inseparable, although particular works are appropriately associated with a Person.

**Texts and voice.** Matthew 3:13–17; Matthew 28:19; CCC 185–197, 232–267; Q5 Basil on the Spirit's gifts. References R4 and the Creed text linked through R0.

**FAQs.** Is this a contradiction? The claim is one divine nature and three Persons, not one and three in the same respect. Is Jesus a lesser god? No; the Son is eternally God, of the same divine nature as the Father. Can water or a shamrock explain it? Such comparisons can mislead by suggesting changing forms or parts; state their limits and prefer the baptismal words.

**Practice and check.** Give each pair three short descriptions: three gods, one actor wearing masks, and one God in three Persons. Use 5 minutes to sort, 5 to explain and 5 to connect the Creed with Baptism. Expected output: corrected statements. Exit check: explain why “God has three parts” fails.

**During the week.** Pray the sign of the cross slowly, or observe its words if not ready to pray.

## Session 04 Creation human dignity sin and grace

**Question and outcome.** Why is a good world also wounded? Participants can distinguish created goodness, original sin and personal sin, and explain grace as God's freely given help and life.

**Essential teaching.** 1. God creates freely from nothing; every human being has inherent dignity. Creation and scientific accounts address different kinds of questions, though their relationship merits thoughtful discussion. 2. Original sin is an inherited deprivation of original holiness, not a personal act committed by an infant; human nature is wounded rather than wholly destroyed. 3. Grace precedes and enables our response. Salvation is God's gift, calling forth faith working through love; it cannot be purchased by accumulated good deeds.

**Texts and voice.** Genesis 1:26–31; Romans 5:12–21; Ephesians 2:8–10; CCC 279–324, 355–421, 1987–2016; Q4 Athanasius on sharing God's life. References R5 and R0.

**FAQs.** Is the body bad? No; bodily life belongs to God's good creation and Christian hope includes resurrection. Does illness prove someone sinned? No; do not infer personal guilt from suffering or disability. Why does God allow evil? Christian faith offers creation, freedom, the cross and resurrection as a framework of hope, not a complete explanation of every tragedy.

**Practice and check.** Use 5 minutes to list signs of goodness and brokenness in an invented community, 5 to distinguish responsibility from misfortune and 5 to propose a grace-enabled response. Expected output: a case analysis without blaming sufferers. Exit check: explain gift and response together.

**During the week.** Notice one gift and perform one small act of care. Personal experiences of trauma are never requested as examples.

## Session 05 Jesus Christ and the work of salvation

**Question and outcome.** Who is Jesus and what does he save us for? Participants can explain that Jesus is one divine Person with a complete divine nature and a complete human nature, and summarise the Paschal mystery.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The eternal Son truly becomes human; he is neither partly God nor a human person later promoted to divinity. 2. His teaching, healing, obedience, cross and bodily resurrection belong together in God's saving work. 3. His once-for-all self-offering reconciles us to God and opens adopted life in him; no single analogy exhausts the atonement. Teach his real death and burial, descent to the dead, resurrection, Ascension and continuing heavenly intercession as the movement of one saving mystery. Jesus' Jewish identity matters. The passion must never be used to assign collective guilt to Jewish people.

**Texts and voice.** John 1:1–18; Philippians 2:5–11; 1 Corinthians 15:3–8; CCC 422–478, 595–618, 624–682; Q4 Athanasius. References R0, R6 and Q4's source.

**FAQs.** Did Jesus merely seem human? No; he truly lived and suffered as man. Does the resurrection mean his ideas survived? Catholic faith proclaims his real bodily resurrection, in a glorified life beyond ordinary resuscitation. Did the Father stop loving the Son on the cross? No; Father and Son are united in the divine saving love, while Christ truly enters human suffering.

**Creed coverage.** Explain the descent as Christ truly entering death and bringing salvation to the just who awaited him, not suffering the punishment of the damned (CCC 632–637). Ascension means his glorified humanity enters heavenly glory; seated at the Father’s right hand expresses his reign and continuing intercession, not retirement from human concerns (CCC 659–667). Christ’s glorious return is revisited in Session 24 (CCC 668–682).

**Practice and check.** Spend 3 minutes correcting “Jesus was half God,” 6 ordering death, descent, resurrection and Ascension, and 6 explaining their meaning in pairs. Expected output: a salvation timeline with one sentence explaining descent and one explaining Ascension and intercession. Exit check: distinguish descent to the dead from the hell of damnation and explain how the risen Christ remains active.

**Pacing note.** Keep the salvation timeline central; specialist comparisons of atonement theories belong in optional depth.

**During the week.** Read one resurrection account and note a detail that surprises you.

## Session 06 The Holy Spirit and Christian transformation

**Question and outcome.** How does God act in us now? Participants can identify the Spirit as a divine Person, describe sanctification and distinguish a spiritual gift from proof of personal superiority.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The Spirit is Lord and giver of life, not an impersonal energy. 2. The Spirit unites us to Christ and forms the Church through Scripture, sacraments, prayer and charity. 3. Gifts are for service and require discernment; growth is recognised through charity and the fruits of the Spirit, not measured by dramatic experiences. Define sanctification as being made holy through God's grace.

**Texts and voice.** Acts 2:1–11; Romans 8:14–17; Galatians 5:22–25; CCC 683–747, 1830–1832; Q5 Basil. Reference R7.

**FAQs.** Must I speak in tongues? No; not all receive the same gifts. Does feeling nothing mean God is absent? No; feelings vary and are not a sacramental validity test or a measure of God's love. Is every strong impression God's instruction? No; test impressions against faith, reason, charity, responsibilities and wise counsel.

**Practice and check.** Use 5 minutes to read the fruits of the Spirit, 5 to discuss an invented case of a gifted but unkind leader and 5 to identify a humble service response. Expected output: three sensible discernment questions. Exit check: name one ordinary way the Spirit acts.

**During the week.** Ask for help to practise one fruit in a specific relationship. Leaders avoid diagnosing experiences or identifying distress as demonic activity.

## Session 07 The Church and belonging to Christ

**Question and outcome.** Why a Church rather than solitary belief? Participants can explain the four marks and the relationship between the whole Church, a diocese and a parish.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The Church is God's people, Christ's body and the Spirit's temple, both visibly structured and a spiritual communion. 2. One, holy, catholic and apostolic name gifts and a calling; holiness does not mean every member or leader acts well. 3. The Catholic Church includes Eastern Catholic Churches. Other Christians share real elements of Christian life; ecumenism seeks unity in truth and charity. Explain the Church's necessity in relation to Christ without claiming all non-Catholics are automatically condemned.

**Texts and voice.** Acts 2:42–47; 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; CCC 748–870; Lumen gentium 8, 14–16; Q6 Ignatius. References R8, R9, R10.

**FAQs.** Is the Church just its clergy? No; all the baptised share its life and mission. How can a holy Church contain abuse? Christ's gifts do not excuse human wrongdoing; truth, justice, protection and repentance are required. Can other Christians be saved? Salvation comes through Christ; Catholic teaching recognises God's grace beyond visible Catholic boundaries and entrusts individuals to his judgment.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes mapping parish and diocese, 5 linking local ministries to the four marks and 5 identifying a safe route for concerns. Expected output: a simple local Church map with verified contacts. Exit check: explain “catholic” as universal.

**During the week.** Observe a parish activity or read about an Eastern Catholic community. No one is pressured to enter a building or meet a leader they do not feel safe with.

## Session 08 The Pope bishops and teaching authority

**Question and outcome.** What is the Pope for? Participants can explain the Bishop of Rome's Petrine ministry, recognise the role of bishops and distinguish infallibility from sinlessness or universal expertise.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The Pope succeeds Peter and serves visible unity; bishops are successors of the apostles, with their own responsibility for their Churches. 2. The Magisterium is the Church's living teaching office, serving the received faith rather than inventing revelation. 3. Papal infallibility applies under defined conditions to definitive teaching on faith or morals for the whole Church; it does not cover every comment. Infallibility also involves the college of bishops and definitive ordinary and universal teaching. Non-definitive authoritative teaching still calls for religious submission of intellect and will; a private opinion is a different category.

**Texts and voice.** Matthew 16:13–19; Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–17; CCC 880–896; Lumen gentium 22–25; Q7 Benedict XVI on the Pope's service. References R10, R11 and Q7's source.

**FAQs.** Can a pope sin? Yes; infallibility is not personal impeccability. Can he change the Gospel? No; his ministry is bound to God's Word. Does every disagreement mean rejection of the Church? Questions, difficulties and the authority of a particular statement require careful distinctions and appropriate guidance; do not reduce all teachings to optional opinion or all questions to disloyalty.

**Practice and check.** In 15 minutes compare labelled examples of a dogmatic definition, an authoritative non-definitive teaching and a personal preference. The author supplies the source, category and rationale in the leader key. Expected output: accurate categories and a question where uncertain. Exit check: explain the limits of infallibility.

**During the week.** Pray for Church leaders and read one paragraph of a papal text in context. Leaders use enduring documents rather than making a living pope's personality the organising principle.

## Session 09 The saints and the communion of saints

**Question and outcome.** What does friendship across death mean? Participants can distinguish adoration of God, honour for saints and requests for intercession, and connect sanctity with ordinary life.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Communion includes sharing in holy things and communion among holy persons, on earth, in purification and in glory. 2. Christ is the one mediator; the prayers of his members participate in his mediation rather than rival it. 3. Canonised saints are recognised examples and intercessors, not flawless authorities on everything. Include women and men, lay and ordained, East and West, and different cultures; choose sourced biographies rather than embellished stories.

**Texts and voice.** Hebrews 12:1–2; Revelation 5:8; 1 Timothy 2:1–5; CCC 946–962, 2683–2684; Q8 Thérèse as an example of accessible holiness. Reference R12.

**FAQs.** Why ask saints when I can pray to God? You can and should pray directly to God; asking saints resembles asking fellow Christians to pray, within Christ's communion. Are statues idols? Images may help remembrance and veneration; they are not gods or magical objects. Must I have a favourite saint? No; particular friendships and devotions are optional.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes comparing a short biography of Thérèse with a lay saint's, 5 identifying a realistic virtue and 5 distinguishing a reliable fact from an unsourced anecdote. Expected output: a small “virtue in ordinary life” plan. Exit check: explain the difference between honour and worship.

**During the week.** Read a short, sourced saint's biography and choose one modest act of love. The writer commissions a geographically varied biography set with sources and image credits.

## Session 10 The Virgin Mary and discipleship

**Question and outcome.** Why does Mary matter to faith in Jesus? Participants can explain the four Marian dogmas and distinguish them from optional devotions and private revelations.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Mother of God affirms that the one Mary bore is truly God the Son incarnate; she is a creature redeemed by Christ, not the source of his divinity. 2. Teach perpetual virginity, Immaculate Conception and Assumption. Immaculate Conception concerns Mary's preservation from original sin from her conception by Christ's merits, not Jesus' virginal conception. The Assumption concerns her being taken body and soul into heavenly glory. 3. Mary's faith and intercession are wholly dependent on Christ; honour for her differs essentially from adoration of God. Rosary, icons and other devotions should lead to Christ.

**Texts and voice.** Luke 1:26–56; John 2:1–11; CCC 484–511, 963–975; Q9 Irenaeus on Mary and Eve. References R13 and R0.

**FAQs.** Do Catholics worship Mary? No; adoration belongs to God alone. Must I accept an apparition? Private revelations add nothing to the deposit of faith and are not binding as articles of faith, even when approved. Does the Bible call Jesus' relatives brothers? It does; explain the Catholic reading alongside wider biblical kinship language, acknowledging that other Christians interpret these passages differently.

**Practice and check.** Use 5 minutes to match each dogma with its meaning, 5 to read Mary's praise of God and 5 to distinguish a dogma, devotion and apparition report. Expected output: a corrected matching sheet. Exit check: explain whose conception “Immaculate Conception” refers to.

**During the week.** Pray or reflect on the Magnificat; optionally explore a decade of the rosary using a free guide.

## Session 11 Prayer as relationship with God

**Question and outcome.** How can I begin praying honestly? Participants can try two forms of prayer and make a small, sustainable plan without equating success with a feeling.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Prayer responds to God's initiative and includes the whole person. 2. Blessing and adoration, petition, intercession, thanksgiving and praise shape Christian prayer; vocal prayer, meditation and contemplation are related expressions. 3. Distraction and dryness need patience rather than performance anxiety. Contemplation is a gift; no technique compels God or guarantees an experience.

**Texts and voice.** Luke 11:1–13; Romans 8:26–27; CCC 2558–2565, 2623–2649, 2700–2724; Q8 Thérèse and Q10 Teresa of Ávila. References R14, R15.

**FAQs.** Are set prayers less authentic? No; inherited words can express and educate genuine prayer. What if my attention wanders? Return gently; the discovery of distraction can itself be a moment of turning back. What if prayer worsens distress? Pause or change the exercise and seek suitable pastoral or professional support; this course does not prescribe treatment or demanding spiritual practices.

**Practice and check.** Offer 3 minutes of a short spoken prayer, 3 of silent attention to a Gospel phrase, 4 for quiet reflection and 5 for making a two-minute daily plan. Listening with eyes open or simply observing is welcome. Expected output: a chosen prayer form and time. Exit check: explain why dryness is not necessarily failure.

**During the week.** Try the two-minute practice on three days and note what helps. Offer the Jesus Prayer, a psalm or thanksgiving as options, without prescribing breathing methods or extended repetition.

## Session 12 The Our Father and a rhythm of prayer

**Question and outcome.** How does Jesus teach us to pray? Participants can explain the Lord's Prayer's seven petitions and build a realistic rhythm of personal and communal prayer.

**Essential teaching.** 1. We pray to the Father with the Son in the Spirit; “our” includes other people and their needs. 2. The petitions move through God's name, kingdom and will to daily bread, forgiveness, temptation and deliverance. God does not tempt us to evil. 3. Scripture, the Liturgy of the Hours, Sunday worship and simple household prayer connect daily life to the Church's prayer. Devotional practices support rather than replace the sacraments.

**Texts and voice.** Matthew 6:5–15; CCC 2759–2865; Q11 Cyprian on common prayer. Reference R16 and Q11's source.

**FAQs.** Must I use old-fashioned English? Approved translations differ; explain the meaning and use the local approved text in common worship. Does forgiveness require returning to an unsafe relationship? No; forgiveness does not remove the need for boundaries, justice or protection. Must I pray for hours? No; start with a sustainable commitment appropriate to your responsibilities.

**Practice and check.** Spend 7 minutes pairing the seven petitions with everyday concerns, 3 praying or listening to the prayer, and 5 creating a weekly rhythm. Expected output: a prayer plan with one realistic daily anchor and a communal option. Exit check: explain “daily bread” beyond one's own needs.

**During the week.** Pray one petition slowly each day. Leaders prepare a large-print prayer sheet or audio alternative; reproduction permissions are checked before release.

## Session 13 The sacramental life and the liturgical year

**Question and outcome.** Why does faith involve water, oil, bread and wine? Participants can name all seven sacraments, group them and distinguish a sacrament from a sacramental.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Sacraments are effective signs of grace instituted by Christ and entrusted to the Church. Christ acts through them; their efficacy does not rest on the minister's personal holiness. 2. Their fruits also involve the recipient's disposition; they are not mechanical magic. Baptism, Confirmation and Orders impart an enduring sacramental character and are not repeated. 3. Initiation, healing and service of communion organise the seven. Blessings and other sacramentals prepare us to receive grace but are distinct. The liturgical year forms us around Christ's mystery.

**Texts and voice.** John 1:14; Romans 6:3–4; CCC 1066–1134, 1163–1173, 1210–1212, 1667–1679; Q12, attributed to Cyril, on learning through experienced worship. Reference R17.

**FAQs.** Why seven? The Church recognises seven sacraments instituted by Christ, rather than treating every religious symbol as a sacrament. Is grace a substance in an object? Grace is God's gift and action, not a measurable fluid. Are Eastern Catholic celebrations different? Their rites differ while celebrating the same sacramental faith; avoid mistaking a Roman custom for the only Catholic practice.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes sorting seven name cards, 5 distinguishing a sacrament from a blessed object and 5 placing major seasons on a simple calendar. Expected output: correct categories and a liturgical-year map. Exit check: give one reason a sacrament is more than a visual reminder.

**During the week.** Notice the coming Sunday's season and one sign used in worship. Use descriptions of sacred objects; never stage a sacrament as a classroom simulation.

## Session 14 Baptism and new life

**Question and outcome.** What does it mean to be born into Christ? Participants can describe the essential celebration, its effects and how an adult or family begins appropriate parish preparation.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Baptism joins us to Christ's death and resurrection, forgives original and personal sin, grants new life and incorporates us into the Church. 2. The essential sign is washing with real water with the Trinitarian baptismal words and the intention to do what the Church does. Ordinary ministers are bishops, priests and deacons in the Latin Church; in necessity anyone with the required intention can baptise. 3. A person not already baptised can receive it. Infant Baptism witnesses to grace's priority and requires subsequent formation; adults prepare through faith, conversion and the Church's initiation process.

**Celebration and preparation.** Explain water and the Trinitarian words first; then the associated anointings, white garment, candle and baptismal promises. Baptism gives an enduring seal, so a valid Christian Baptism is not repeated. Uncertainty about an earlier Baptism belongs with the parish, not a group vote. Eastern initiation commonly joins Baptism, Chrismation and Eucharist together. Sources: CCC 1213–1284 [R18].

**Texts and voice.** Matthew 28:18–20; Romans 6:3–11; Q12, attributed to Cyril, used as a historical witness to formation around Baptism, not as a modern ritual manual.

**FAQs.** Why baptise infants? God's gift precedes mature understanding, while parents and community undertake formation in faith. Does Baptism remove every struggle? No; weakness, suffering and temptation remain. What about those who die without Baptism? Teach its necessity alongside Baptism of blood, desire and God's freedom; the Church entrusts unbaptised infants to God's mercy and has hope for their salvation, rather than asserting their condemnation.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes identifying the font and signs through a picture or visit, 5 tracing “gift and lifelong growth,” and 5 planning an appropriate parish enquiry. Expected output: an annotated signs sheet and a preparation contact. Exit check: distinguish Baptism's effects from later learning.

**During the week.** Reflect on baptismal promises or discuss a Baptism enquiry privately. No class exercise pours water while reciting a baptismal formula over someone.

## Session 15 Confirmation and the gift of the Spirit

**Question and outcome.** How are we strengthened for Christian witness? Participants can explain Confirmation's relation to Baptism and identify its essential signs, effects and preparation route.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Confirmation deepens baptismal grace and strengthens our bond with Christ and the Church for witness. 2. Explain laying on of hands, anointing with sacred chrism and the sacramental words in the relevant approved rite; distinguish the whole ritual from its essential elements. 3. It is received once by a baptised person not yet confirmed. In the Latin Church the bishop is the ordinary minister and priests may confirm under specified faculties; in Eastern Catholic Churches priests normally chrismate with the prescribed chrism. Age and preparation practices differ.

**Celebration and preparation.** Explain the enduring character, prayer, suitable formation and the sponsor's role. For those with the use of reason, preparation includes understanding, an intention to receive and appropriate disposition; leaders refer individual circumstances to the parish. Sacred chrism is perfumed oil consecrated for sacramental use. Sources: CCC 1285–1321 [R19].

**Texts and voice.** Acts 8:14–17; Acts 19:1–7; Q5 Basil on the Spirit and adoption.

**FAQs.** Is this graduation? No; Christian initiation leads into continuing life in Christ. Does it mean the Spirit was absent before? No; the Spirit already acts in Baptism and in the person's life. Why do some babies receive it? Eastern initiation shows that this is God's gift, not a reward for adolescent independence.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes comparing a Latin and an Eastern initiation timeline, 5 identifying sacramental signs and 5 drafting a small act of Christian witness. Expected output: an accurately labelled timeline and service commitment. Exit check: explain why “choosing my religion for the first time” is incomplete as a definition.

**During the week.** Ask for courage to carry out one act of faithful service. Show an image or a written description of chrism; do not imitate the sacramental anointing.

## Session 16 The Eucharist and the real presence

**Question and outcome.** What does the Church believe it receives in Holy Communion? Participants can explain real presence, sacrifice and communion, and identify the usual preparation for receiving.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The Eucharist makes sacramentally present Christ's one sacrifice; he is not crucified again. 2. By consecration the whole substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood while their appearances remain. Transubstantiation names this real change; the whole living Christ is present under either species. 3. Communion unites us with Christ and his body, the Church, and commits us to charity. Christ is present in several ways in the liturgy; his Eucharistic presence is unique.

**Celebration and preparation.** A validly ordained bishop or priest consecrates. The celebration uses wheat bread and natural grape wine according to the Church's requirements; lay ministers may distribute Communion but do not consecrate. Ordinarily Catholic recipients must be properly prepared and disposed. Someone conscious of grave sin should receive sacramental absolution first, subject to the Church's stated exceptional conditions. For the Latin Church explain the usual one-hour fast before reception, with water and medicine excepted; the elderly, sick and those caring for them have the exemptions stated in canon 919. Eastern fasting discipline differs. Refer a person conscious of grave sin to the priest: canon 916 specifies the exceptional conjunction of grave reason, no opportunity to confess, and perfect contrition including the intention to confess as soon as possible. Attendance never creates an obligation to receive. Explain the Church’s precept to receive Communion at least annually, ordinarily during the Easter season, after preparation and subject to the conditions and exceptions stated in canon 920; it does not authorise unprepared reception. Sources: CCC 1322–1419, especially 1374–1377 and 1385–1390 [R20].

**Texts and voice.** John 6:51–58; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 11:23–29; Q13 Justin, with its second-century context.

**FAQs.** Is it only a symbol? It is a sacramental sign that truly contains Christ, not merely a reminder. Is Jesus divided when the host is broken? No; Christ is whole under each species and each part. Can every attendee receive? No; explain Catholic discipline respectfully, acknowledge narrowly defined exceptions through competent ministers and offer a clear way to remain seated without embarrassment.

**Practice and check.** Use 5 minutes to distinguish appearance and sacramental reality, 5 to correct “Jesus is sacrificed again,” and 5 to discuss a fictional visitor's participation. Expected output: a respectful visitor explanation. Exit check: explain why receiving under one species does not mean receiving half of Christ.

**During the week.** Spend a short time in prayer before the tabernacle or reflect on John 6. Eucharistic exposition requires appropriate parish arrangements; it is not a classroom prop.

## Session 17 Understanding and participating in the Mass

**Question and outcome.** What are we doing together on Sunday? Participants can follow the main movement of Mass and connect Word, offering, thanksgiving, Communion and mission.

**Essential teaching.** 1. The liturgy of the Word and liturgy of the Eucharist form one act of worship, framed by introductory and concluding rites. 2. The assembly listens and responds; the ordained celebrant has a distinct sacramental role. Explain the homily, intercessions, preparation of gifts, Eucharistic Prayer, Communion rite and dismissal. 3. Participation is inward and outward, not measured by constant speaking, physical ability or a particular emotional response. Connect Sunday and holy-day worship with Christ's resurrection and the Church's obligation, while explaining legitimate excuses such as illness and necessary care. Check the local holy-day calendar rather than assuming all countries observe the same days.

**Texts and voice.** Luke 24:28–35; Acts 20:7; CCC 1345–1355, 2180–2183; Q13 Justin and First Apology 67 as a further-reading reference. His account witnesses to continuity without proving every modern rubric existed in the second century. Sources R20, R21.

**FAQs.** Why repeat a liturgy? Repetition trains attention and shared worship, as repeated meals sustain life. What if I cannot stand or kneel? Participate as you can; disability or illness is not irreverence. What can a newcomer do? Listen, follow comfortable responses and remain seated at Communion; parish customs about approaching for a blessing vary, so explain the local practice rather than assuming it.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes ordering the main movements, 5 following a parish-approved worship aid and 5 preparing a visitor's plan. Expected output: an annotated Mass map. Exit check: connect the dismissal with weekday discipleship.

**During the week.** Attend or observe Mass if willing, or use an accessible description. Arrange any church tour in advance; give an Eastern Divine Liturgy comparison as optional deeper learning.

## Session 18 Reconciliation mercy and truthful return

**Question and outcome.** How do we return after sin? Participants can describe a first or returning confession, distinguish mortal and venial sin, and identify a trusted parish route without disclosing sins to the group.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Christ gives the Church a sacramental ministry of forgiveness and reconciliation. 2. The penitent's acts are contrition, confession and satisfaction; the priest gives sacramental absolution. Contrition includes sorrow for sin and a purpose of amendment; imperfect contrition disposes us to receive forgiveness in the sacrament. 3. Mortal sin requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent. The sacrament restores grace lost through mortal sin, reconciles with the Church and strengthens conversion; regular confession of venial sins is recommended.

**Celebration and preparation.** Only a priest with the required faculty absolves. Outline a private examination, greeting and sign of the cross, confession, counsel and penance, act of contrition and absolution, followed by carrying out the penance. Grave sins remembered after examination are confessed in kind and number as far as possible; honest forgetfulness differs from deliberate concealment. Explain the absolute sacramental seal and distinguish it from the limited confidentiality of an ordinary group discussion. Sources: CCC 1422–1498 [R22].

**Texts and voice.** Luke 15:11–32; John 20:19–23; Q14 Francis on the Father's mercy.

**FAQs.** Why confess to a priest? Christ's forgiveness is given through his minister and visibly reconciles us with the Church. What if I cannot remember the words? Tell the priest; he can guide you. What if I repeatedly fear I have confessed badly? Seek a consistent, trusted confessor and appropriate support; the course should not encourage repetitive checking or detailed public examinations.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes reading a fictional “I have not been for years” dialogue, 5 arranging the steps on a card and 5 explaining what a penitent can ask for. Expected output: a private preparation guide. No one plays a confessor or gives mock absolution. Exit check: name the three conditions for mortal sin.

**Church precepts.** Explain the obligation to confess grave sins at least annually once bound by the precept; distinguish that minimum from the recommendation of regular confession. Connect the Eucharistic precept with Session 16 and the summary in Session 22 (CCC 2042).

**During the week.** Make a gentle private examen; arrange a conversation or confession if ready and eligible. Reconciliation with God does not remove obligations to repair harm or seek justice.

## Session 19 Anointing of the Sick and care at the end of life

**Question and outcome.** How does Christ accompany serious illness? Participants can distinguish Anointing, pastoral care and Viaticum, and know how to request a priest in good time.

**Essential teaching.** 1. This sacrament is for baptised faithful whose health is seriously impaired through illness or old age; it is not reserved for the final minutes of life or applied indiscriminately to every minor illness. 2. A bishop or priest administers it through the Church's prayer and anointing with oil. It gives strength, peace, courage, union with Christ's passion and forgiveness of sins when the sick person cannot obtain it through Penance; bodily healing may be given if conducive to salvation. 3. Viaticum is Eucharistic Communion for the passage towards death; “last rites” may involve several distinct sacraments and prayers.

**Celebration and preparation.** Describe prayer, laying on of hands and anointing of forehead and hands in the Latin rite; other rites differ. It can be repeated when serious illness recurs or the condition worsens. Contact the parish or hospital chaplain; do not wait unnecessarily. Individual eligibility, including mental illness, requires the priest's pastoral judgment rather than a course checklist. Sources: CCC 1499–1532 [R23].

**Texts and voice.** James 5:14–15; Mark 6:12–13; Q14 Francis, applied to Christ's compassionate presence, not a promise of medical cure.

**FAQs.** Does requesting it mean giving up? No; it accompanies treatment and hope. Will it guarantee recovery? No; God's grace is not a guarantee of a particular medical outcome. What if the priest cannot arrive immediately? Continue ordinary prayer and care, contact the appropriate chaplaincy route and follow medical advice; laypeople can pray but cannot administer this sacrament.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes distinguishing three fictional care situations, 5 locating the parish and hospital chaplain routes and 5 composing a compassionate request. Expected output: a contact card, checked by the local organiser before distribution. Exit check: explain why Anointing and Viaticum are different.

**During the week.** Offer a practical act of support with consent. No uninvited visits, promises of healing or classroom imitations of anointing.

## Session 20 Holy Orders and the service of communion

**Question and outcome.** How does ordained ministry serve the whole Church? Participants can distinguish bishop, priest and deacon, and describe how Holy Orders relates to everyone's baptismal vocation.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Holy Orders continues the apostolic ministry in three degrees: episcopate, presbyterate and diaconate. 2. Bishops and priests share ministerial priesthood; deacons are ordained for service, including word, liturgy and charity, rather than ordained to the priesthood. 3. The essential sign is the bishop's laying on of hands with the consecratory prayer. It confers grace and an enduring character; the sacrament does not make someone morally superior or above accountability.

**Celebration and preparation.** Only a validly ordained bishop confers Orders. Catholic teaching reserves sacred ordination to baptised men; there is no personal entitlement to ordination. Explain vocational discernment, formation, episcopal admission and the distinction between sacramental teaching and disciplines such as priestly celibacy. Latin priests are normally celibate; married priests exist in Eastern Catholic Churches and under certain Latin provisions. Bishops are chosen from celibate men. Religious profession and consecrated life are distinct from ordination. Sources: CCC 1536–1600 [R24].

**Texts and voice.** Mark 10:42–45; 1 Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:6; Q6 Ignatius for early episcopal communion, read within its historical context.

**FAQs.** Is the ordained priesthood the only vocation? No; baptismal holiness and mission belong to all, in many states of life. Why are women not ordained? Present the Church's stated teaching and its account of the apostolic pattern and Christ's action, without implying lesser dignity or fewer gifts; allow serious questions. Does ordination excuse abuse? Never; sacramental character does not cancel safeguarding, law or accountability.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes matching the three degrees to their ministries, 5 distinguishing Orders from religious vows and 5 mapping ways lay and ordained members collaborate. Expected output: a ministry map. Exit check: explain why a monk is not necessarily a priest and why a deacon cannot consecrate the Eucharist.

**During the week.** Pray for vocations and identify a form of service appropriate to your own life. Optional guest testimony must leave time for questions.

## Session 21 Matrimony family and faithful love

**Question and outcome.** What does the Church mean by marriage as a sacrament? Participants can describe covenant, free consent, permanence, fidelity and openness to life, and identify pastoral help for complex situations.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Marriage is a covenant between a man and a woman ordered to the spouses' good and the generation and education of children; between the baptised a valid marriage is a sacrament. 2. Free consent, the absence of impediments and observance of applicable canonical requirements matter; a wedding ceremony alone does not resolve every question of validity. 3. Teach unity, indissolubility, mutual self-giving, chastity within one's state of life and responsible parenthood. Explain Catholic teaching on the inseparability of the unitive and procreative meanings of marital intercourse and its rejection of contraception, with sensitive, sourced further formation.

**Celebration and preparation.** In the Latin tradition spouses confer the sacrament on one another through consent before the Church's authorised witness and witnesses, subject to legal exceptions. In Eastern Catholic traditions the priest's blessing has a distinct constitutive role; obtain a qualified review of the relevant rite. Marriage preparation includes freedom, communication, faith and responsibilities. Infertility does not itself invalidate marriage. Sources: CCC 1601–1666, 2360–2379 [R25].

**Texts and voice.** Genesis 2:18–24; John 2:1–11; Ephesians 5:21–33, explained through mutual self-giving rather than domination; Q2 Benedict XVI on encounter and his wider teaching on love in R2.

**FAQs.** Is an annulment a Catholic divorce? No; a declaration of nullity judges whether a valid marriage came into being. Must someone remain in an unsafe home? No; protection and separation can be necessary. Can divorced people come to church? Yes; distinguish civil divorce, a subsequent union and individual sacramental questions, which need informed pastoral accompaniment rather than public judgment.

**Practice and check.** Use 5 minutes to identify free consent in fictional cases, 5 to distinguish permanence from tolerating abuse and 5 to locate preparation or tribunal guidance. Expected output: a safe referral map. Exit check: explain why inability to conceive does not mean inability to marry.

**During the week.** Practise one act of faithful care in a family or friendship. Single, widowed, divorced and LGBTQ participants should be addressed respectfully; nobody's personal circumstances become a debate exercise.

## Session 22 Conscience the commandments and the Beatitudes

**Question and outcome.** How does grace shape everyday choices? Participants can use a basic moral reasoning process, describe conscience and connect commandments with the Beatitudes and virtue.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Christian morality responds to grace and aims at love of God and neighbour. The commandments protect these relationships; the Beatitudes show the shape of life in Christ. 2. Conscience makes a practical judgment about a concrete choice. A person must follow its certain judgment and also take responsibility for forming it through truth, prayer, reason, Scripture and the Church's teaching. Error may be culpable or nonculpable; absence of personal guilt does not make an objectively wrongful act good. 3. Ask what is being chosen, why and in what circumstances. A good intention does not make an intrinsically wrongful choice good. Distinguish the act, the person's responsibility and their enduring dignity.

**Manageable teaching sequence.** Within the 20-minute Discovery, use 4 minutes for the commandments map, 4 for the Beatitudes, 6 for conscience and 6 for the practical decision process. These four-minute segments provide orientation, not exhaustive treatment. Grace has already been introduced in Session 04, growth in holiness and the fruits of the Spirit in Session 06, prayer in Sessions 11–12, culpability in Session 18 and marriage in Session 21; recap these rather than introducing every field afresh. Name faith, hope and charity and the four cardinal virtues on a reference sheet, with definitions, rather than attempting seven additional mini-lectures. For a group without this foundation, split Session 22 into two 90-minute meetings: 22A commandments and Beatitudes, 22B conscience and choices. This gives 25 meetings while retaining the same 24 numbered topics.

**Commissioned commandments map.** Use these short paraphrases as headings, with the approved Catechism text available to the writer: 1 worship God alone; 2 honour God's name; 3 keep the Lord's day; 4 honour parents and family responsibilities; 5 protect human life; 6 live sexual fidelity and chastity; 7 respect others' goods and economic justice; 8 tell the truth and protect a neighbour's reputation; 9 cultivate purity of heart in sexual desire; 10 resist envy and disordered desire for possessions. Present 1–3 under love of God and 4–10 under love of neighbour, without claiming the two loves can be separated. Read all eight Beatitudes in Matthew 5:3–12; highlight mercy, peacemaking and purity of heart as examples of positive discipleship, not as a replacement list of commandments. The learner output is one concrete link between a commandment, a Beatitude and an act of love.

**Texts and voice.** Exodus 20:1–17; Matthew 5:1–12; Mark 12:28–34; CCC 1716–1729, 1750–1756, 1776–1802, 1803–1829, 2041–2043, 2052–2082; Q15 Leo the Great on dignity. References R26 and R0.

**FAQs.** Is conscience whatever I feel? No; it involves an honest judgment that should be informed and examined. Do rules earn salvation? No; grace comes first, and moral growth is our enabled response. What about sexuality or contested teachings? Explain the Church's teaching clearly and compassionately, distinguishing persons from acts and basic dignity from moral approval; provide optional, reviewed learning and private pastoral guidance without humiliating participants.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes mapping the commandments to love of God and neighbour, 5 analysing an invented workplace dishonesty case and 5 proposing a virtuous alternative. Expected output: act, intention, circumstances and next step. Exit check: explain why a helpful motive does not justify every method.

**During the week.** Practise one virtue in one specific decision. The writer commissions further-learning sheets on life ethics, sexuality, money, truthfulness and digital behaviour; these are free, not a paid extension.

**Minimum briefs for the five further-learning sheets.** Each receives a plain-language summary, two FAQs, one everyday case, a practical next step and a qualified doctrinal review. Distribute them as a free reference pack; they are not five extra talks to squeeze into this meeting.

**Practical Church precepts map.** The free leader and learner pack includes a one-page reference to CCC 2041–2043: Sunday and holy-day Mass with legitimate excuses (Session 17); annual confession of grave sins as applicable (Session 18); annual Communion, ordinarily in the Easter season, with proper preparation (Session 16); prescribed fasting and abstinence, with age limits, exemptions and local rules verified for the course's location; and material support for the Church according to one's ability. These are minimum practices within a life of love, not a complete definition of holiness. The local supplement supplies the current calendar and fasting rules; this framework does not invent universal dates or treat voluntary devotions as obligations.

## Session 23 Catholic social teaching and works of mercy

**Question and outcome.** What does love require in public life? Participants can explain dignity, solidarity, subsidiarity and the common good, and plan a modest action with affected people's input.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Human dignity applies from conception to natural death and includes the vulnerable, poor, disabled, migrant and excluded. 2. Solidarity means responsibility for one another; subsidiarity protects the proper agency of persons and smaller communities while requiring larger bodies to help when needed. The common good concerns conditions that allow people and communities to flourish. 3. Works of mercy, just treatment of workers, peace and care for creation express discipleship. Distinguish binding moral principles from prudential disagreement about policies; the faith cannot be identified with one political party.

**Texts and voice.** Matthew 25:31–46; Luke 10:25–37; James 2:14–17; CCC 1877–1948, 2401–2463; Deus caritas est 25; Q14 Francis as the invitation to embody received mercy. References R27, R2.

**FAQs.** Is charity enough if systems are unjust? Immediate help and work for justice belong together. Does subsidiarity mean governments should never act? No; larger bodies have a duty to support and act where lower levels cannot adequately protect the common good. Must Catholics agree on every policy? No; they must respect moral principles, evidence and the dignity of those affected while discerning practical means.

**Facilitator preparation.** Before the meeting, obtain a brief from an existing local organisation describing a need and the help it actually welcomes. Use an anonymised fictional case if no partner is available.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes understanding the prearranged brief from the local organisation, 5 testing a proposed response against the four principles and 5 assigning a small next step. Expected output: a one-page plan with an owner, a consent step and a realistic date. Exit check: show how solidarity and subsidiarity can support one another.

**During the week.** Carry out or arrange one agreed act of mercy. Check transport, accessibility, safeguarding and consent with the host organisation; no photographs or stories are shared without permission.

## Session 24 Resurrection Christian hope and continuing discipleship

**Question and outcome.** What are we hoping for, and how do we go on? Participants can distinguish resurrection, heaven, purgatory, hell and final judgment, and choose a realistic next step after the course.

**Essential teaching.** 1. Christian hope includes the resurrection of the body and a renewed creation, not merely escape into a disembodied existence. 2. After death comes particular judgment; heaven is communion with God, purgatory the final purification of those assured of salvation, and hell definitive self-exclusion from communion with God. Final judgment manifests God's justice and the consequences of our lives. 3. Christ will return in glory to judge the living and the dead; his return is linked to the resurrection and final judgment, completing his kingdom rather than beginning another ordinary earthly political reign (CCC 668–682). Hope calls us to prayer, conversion, service and witness now. Do not predict dates, declare particular people damned or invent assurances about individuals; distinguish this restraint from the Church’s recognition of canonised saints in glory.

**Texts and voice.** 1 Corinthians 15:20–28, 42–49; Revelation 21:1–5; Matthew 28:16–20; CCC 668–682, 988–1065, 1817–1821; Q1 Augustine, returning to rest in God. References R28 and R0.

**FAQs.** Is purgatory a second chance to reject or accept God? No; it is purification of those who die in God's grace. Can we know that a particular person is in hell? The course should not pronounce such judgments; entrust people to God's justice and mercy. Does hope make present suffering unimportant? No; resurrection gives bodily life and acts of love lasting significance.

**Practice and check.** Spend 5 minutes making a Creed map from memory with support, 5 clarifying one outstanding question and 5 choosing a next step in prayer, community or service. Expected output: a one-month discipleship plan and a course feedback form. Exit check: explain resurrection, Christ’s glorious return and why purgatory differs from hell.

**During the following month.** Offer an optional reunion. Those exploring initiation meet the parish team; others can join a Scripture group, prayer group or service activity. Avoid presenting religious life, ordination or marriage as an expected course outcome.

## G3 Accessibility and facilitator support

Write each main learner sheet in plain English, normally 600–900 words with short paragraphs, three essential points, three FAQs and space for a question. The completed session can have a 1200–1800-word leader script plus optional background. These are production targets, not a requirement to read everything aloud. Explain a technical word at first use and repeat its definition when it returns after several weeks.

Provide selectable text, a structured HTML version, a tagged PDF or accessible equivalent, an audio reading or transcript, and a large-print version. Test actual files with keyboard navigation and a screen reader rather than relying on a claimed standard. Use at least 12-point print body text, generous spacing and high contrast; large print starts at 16–18 points, adjusted to readers' needs. Captions and transcripts are required for teaching videos. Images need meaningful alternative text or captions where they convey content.

Offer listening, speaking, writing or quiet observation as equivalent ways of taking part. Do not require reading aloud, closed eyes, kneeling, disclosure of conversion stories or public explanations of marital, sexual or medical circumstances. Optional activities must include a seated and remote alternative where practical. Let people take breaks and leave without explaining why. Apply the host parish's safeguarding arrangements; never describe group discussion as absolutely confidential.

**Working glossary.** A creed is a shared statement of faith. Revelation is God's making himself known. Tradition is the handing on of apostolic faith; a custom is a particular practice. Grace is God's freely given help and gift of participation in his life. A mystery is a divine reality genuinely revealed but not exhausted by our understanding. A sacrament is an effective sign of grace instituted by Christ and entrusted to the Church. Liturgy is the Church's public worship. A diocese is a local Church entrusted to a bishop. The Magisterium is the Church's living teaching office. Dogma is a truth the Church definitively proposes as divinely revealed. Veneration is honour; adoration is worship owed to God. A charism is a gift given for service. Mystagogy means growing in understanding of the mysteries through the worship and life into which one has been initiated.

**Facilitator pack.** Include a timed plan, plain-language answer key, quotation context, relevant CCC passages, accessibility options, sensitive-topic notes and local referral blanks. Before a meeting, check any local statements about sacramental preparation, Mass schedules, fasting exceptions or chaplaincy contacts. Do not resolve complex canonical questions through a general FAQ.

**Learning checks.** Use low-pressure retrieval after sessions 6, 12, 18 and 24: explain one central teaching, correct one common misconception and choose one appropriate practical step. Leaders use an answer key and invite further questions. Do not grade prayer, confession attendance, emotional disclosures or personal holiness.

## G5 Open publishing and optional print materials

**Proposed model.** Release original course prose, worksheets and editable design sources under CC BY-SA 4.0. This permits adaptation and commercial redistribution with attribution and the applicable ShareAlike conditions. It therefore permits other people to print and sell copies too. If that freedom is unacceptable, reconsider the business model rather than calling a non-commercial or no-derivatives restriction fully open. This is a recommendation for the publisher to adopt, not a declaration that the draft's third-party material has been relicensed [R30].

Keep any future website or tooling code under a suitable separate software licence, such as MIT, and distinguish that from the content licence. The repository should contain a licence file, third-party notices, contribution instructions, a quotation and image rights register, a changelog and reproducible export instructions. Use ordinary editable text as the canonical content; allow DOCX, HTML and print-ready PDFs to be generated from it. A complete course must remain usable offline and without an account.

**Free provision.** Supply learner sheets, facilitator scripts, answer keys, transcripts, prayer guides, source references, local-print PDFs and accessible alternatives. Optional print products may include a bound learner book, a durable leader book, large-print editions or a parish pack of the same worksheets. Charge for manufacturing, binding, distribution and convenience; do not conceal essential answers or deeper doctrinal teaching behind the print purchase. Avoid suggesting payment grants privileged access to grace or ecclesial standing.

**Rights register.** Record a decision for every Scripture quotation, patristic translation, modern papal extract, liturgical text, photograph, icon, font and illustration. Link to restricted material or paraphrase with attribution where appropriate rather than copying it extensively. Ancient authorship does not make a modern translation public domain. Do not distribute a modern Bible or liturgical translation as open content without permission. In an openly editable edition, clearly mark third-party exceptions so downstream users know what they may reuse. Production rights decisions remain a separate pre-publication task; this framework makes no global clearance claim.

**Ecclesial review.** Arrange a qualified Catholic theological review and contact the local ordinary's office about the approval required for publication of catechetical writings under canon 827 §1. Prayer books and reproductions of liturgical material may have additional requirements under canon 826. Do not advertise the course as officially approved, or print an imprimatur or nihil obstat, unless actually granted. Under canon 829, approval of an original text does not automatically extend to new editions or translations. Determine the applicable review for each new edition or translation with the competent authority. The maintained CORE release process does not remove downstream users’ copyright freedoms under its content licence. An AI critic's scores do not provide ecclesiastical approval [R29].

**Community maintenance.** Accept corrections through a public issue process, require contributors to have the rights to their submissions, and review changes before merging into a maintained release. Keep a stable, dated teaching edition alongside work in progress. Distinguish doctrinal correction, pedagogical improvement, translation and local adaptation in the changelog. Invite Eastern Catholic and accessibility reviewers where their knowledge is needed. A local supplement can supply parish contacts and customs without altering universal teaching.

## G6 Production plan and pilot

Start with a modest pilot before writing or printing the entire course. The stages below are a proposed sequence, not a promised timetable. Estimate effort and set dates once collaborators and approval routes are known; ecclesial review and permissions have their own timescales. The named roles may be fulfilled by a small team, but doctrinal review should involve someone other than the main writer.

| Stage | Owner | Work and concrete output | Completion test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Scope and review route | Mark and a parish catechetical adviser | Confirm adult audience, rite, intended use, course outcome and contact with the ordinary's office | Written brief and publication-review route agreed |
| 2 Authoring pattern | Mark | Draft Sessions 01, 03, 10 and 16 as complete learner and leader packs | All four have timed plans, FAQ answers, activities and source entries |
| 3 Content and rights check | Qualified reviewer and rights editor | Check doctrine, quotation wording and proposed reusable editions; identify permissions needed | No unresolved doctrinal correction; no unlabelled third-party material |
| 4 Accessibility check | Accessibility tester and two representative readers | Try print and digital files, glossary, audio or transcript and alternative activities | Readers can find the main point and complete the practical task without inaccessible steps |
| 5 Supervised small pilot | Facilitator and helper | Trial the four selected sessions with roughly 6–10 adults, subject to the agreed ecclesial review arrangements | Actual timings logged; misunderstandings and pastoral concerns recorded without unnecessary personal data |
| 6 Revise and complete | Mark and reviewers | Fix pilot issues, then write the other sessions in batches of four | Each batch meets the same learning and review gates |
| 7 Publication readiness | Publisher and competent ecclesiastical contacts | Complete permissions, required approval process, accessible exports and stable versioning | Release checklist signed off; approval claims match what has actually been granted |
| 8 Print proof and release | Publisher and printer | Proof a learner book and leader book against the free version | Text parity, legibility, binding and total delivered cost checked before ordering |

**Pilot measures.** After each session ask participants to explain the central idea in one sentence, identify one unanswered question and say whether the practice was feasible. Invite feedback on clarity and welcome without soliciting private religious or medical history. Record where the meeting ran over time. With a small pilot, treat numbers as signals rather than evidence of broad effectiveness. A useful initial gate is that at least four of five participants who choose to answer can explain the central distinction, with all substantive doctrinal misunderstandings prompting review.

**First four writing commissions.** Session 01 tests welcome and tone; Session 03 tests the explanation of difficult doctrine; Session 10 tests ecumenical and devotional distinctions; Session 16 tests sacramental precision. If only one full session can be written first, choose Session 01 and build the reusable authoring pattern from it. A parish should not use isolated pilot meetings as sufficient sacramental preparation.

**Print economics.** Obtain quotes for one proof and several small quantities, then calculate net receipts minus printing, packing, payment fees, postage, replacements and any applicable taxes. Do not set prices or order substantial stock from unverified estimates. Consider print on demand or parish pre-orders after a proof has been approved. All print content should identify the free digital equivalent and the edition date.

**Release checklist.** Every session has a plain-language outcome, three essential ideas, defined terms, checked Scripture and CCC references, a verified quotation, three FAQ answers, a timed activity, an answer key, an accessible alternative and a next step. Separately check theological review, rights, the applicable ecclesial approval, document accessibility and print proofing. Track changes publicly and review any time-sensitive local guidance before each course run.

## G7 Independent critic review

The review rubric scores this framework as a planning document, not as completed learner materials. Accuracy means fidelity to Catholic teaching, careful historical attribution and clear source or review limits. Accessibility means understandable structure, defined vocabulary and feasible adaptations for adults without prior knowledge. Actionability means a writer or facilitator can identify what to produce, teach, do and check. Scores above 4.0 out of 5 are required for every dimension of every main section and session; averages cannot conceal a weak section.

**Scoring anchors.** Use the following anchors in each dimension; intermediate scores in 0.1 increments express a reviewer's judgment, not statistical precision. A score of 5.0 means exemplary performance for the stated planning scope, not exhaustive treatment of theology.

| Dimension | 3.0 requires substantial revision | 4.0 competent but acceptance blocked | 4.5 strong planning brief |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | Material errors, misleading attribution or major doctrinal omissions | Generally sound, but an important qualification or required topic is missing | Faithful, appropriately qualified and traceable to named sources; no unresolved substantive error |
| Accessibility | Assumes specialist knowledge or excludes common participation needs | Mostly clear, but density, vocabulary or pacing creates a material barrier | Clear essentials, explained terms, realistic pacing and practical participation alternatives |
| Actionability | Readers cannot identify what to write, teach or do | Useful direction, but a key teaching commission, activity or decision process is underspecified | Observable outcome, sufficient content brief, named inputs, feasible task, check and next step |

**Review procedure.** Freeze and label a draft; give the critic the user requirements and all main sections. Score G1–G7 and Sessions 01–24 separately in all three dimensions. Every score must exceed 4.0. Any score at or below 4.0 blocks acceptance; no average or strong section can compensate. For each blocking issue record section and paragraph, the problem, the source where relevant, its effect on a learner or writer, the proposed correction and the score affected. Legitimate later production tasks may remain open if the framework clearly commissions their content, owner and completion test. An omitted essential doctrine, an unusable task or a missing required decision rule is not merely later production work.

**Revision loop.** The main writer records each issue as accepted and corrected, replaced by a justified alternative, or unresolved. The same critic rechecks revised sections and any related material for regressions, and returns a complete score matrix or explicitly reconfirms unchanged scores. If unavailable, a replacement independent critic receives the full evidence and revision record. Continue until all cells exceed 4.0. Keep the review record with version, reviewer, issue, disposition and re-review result. Request a qualified human theological review independently of this editorial loop.

**Issue log template.** Version; section and location; criterion and score; evidence or source; consequence; proposed correction; writer's disposition; revised version; re-review score; reviewer and date.
